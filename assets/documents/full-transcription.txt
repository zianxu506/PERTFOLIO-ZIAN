I came to SCI-Arc with an interest in architec­
ture, but not a clear idea of what it could be be­
yond finished buildings. Living in Downtown 
Los Angeles started to shift that. The city’s 
density, infrastructure, and constant movement 
made me more aware of how space is shaped 
by use, circulation, and change, rather than fixed 
form.
In the studio, I began to understand architecture 
as a process. I moved between drawing, model­
ing, and making, using both digital and physical 
tools to test ideas. Some projects were abstract, 
translating images into 3D forms or using simple 
operations to generate spatial models, while oth­
ers focused on site and program, like an elemen­
tary school or a public pool. Working between 
these approaches made the process feel iterative 
rather than linear.
Material and environmental thinking also be­
came important. Through fabrication, I worked 
with papier-mâché and casting, eventually 
building a full-scale chair. In another studio, I 
mapped flora and fauna and used that to devel­
op a fitness center that integrates wind energy. 
These projects pushed me to think about archi­
tecture as part of larger environmental systems. 
At the same time, I explored how to represent 
work through photography and layout, using 
tools like robotic cameras to capture models un­
der different lighting. Looking back, these expe­
riences are less about final outcomes and more 
about how I think and work.
In the future, I want to continue working this 
way, developing projects that connect environ­
mental systems, material experimentation, and 
spatial design.

In this studio, we explore how architecture can 
respond to ecological and environmental con­
ditions within complex urban contexts. The site 
is analyzed through its flora and fauna, using 
graphics and color mapping to represent spe­
cies and environmental zones. Precedents are 
studied and reinterpreted with techniques like 
pixel sorting, generating abstract images that 
translate environmental data into visual form. 
These images guide the development of initial 
3D-printed models, which are then refined into 
a fitness center that responds to both program 
and environment. The design incorporates sus­
tainable strategies, including wind energy gen­
eration. Unreal Engine renders visualize the 
building in its context, highlighting architec­
ture as part of a larger ecological system con­
necting design, energy, and landscape.

In this course, we focus on how architectural 
work is documented, curated, and presented 
through the format of a portfolio. As students, 
we learn to organize projects from different 
studios and courses into a cohesive body of 
work, thinking about the portfolio not just as 
a collection, but as a way to communicate our 
own design approach and ideas. A key part of 
the class is the “Capture” section, where we 
photograph our models using a robotic arm in 
the Robot House. By adjusting lighting, back­
grounds, and camera angles, we produce a large 
series of images that reveal different views, 
materials, and colors of each model, helping 
us understand how representation can shape 
perception. Alongside this, we also participate 
in discussions about image-making and visual 
storytelling, exploring how sequencing, fram­
ing, and narrative can influence the way a proj­
ect is read. Through these combined exercis­
es, the course helps us develop both technical 
skills and a clearer sense of authorship in how 
we present our work.

In this design studio, we explore architecture 
through the relationship between interior and 
exterior space, focusing on how schools can 
be rethought under changing urban and envi­
ronmental conditions. As students, we work 
through a series of projects that build on each 
other. The first project is a material “sand­
wich,” where we use found materials to stack 
and assemble layers, helping us understand 
spatial relationships and material logic in a 
hands-on way. The second project is a prece­
dent study of the Centro Direzionale Subway 
Station in Naples, where we analyze drawings 
and reconstruct the project as a 3D model to 
better understand its spatial and structural sys­
tem. In the final project, we combine what we 
learned from the first two exercises to design 
an elementary school in Los Angeles, using 
site observations to organize the building as 
an open, landscape-like system. Through this 
process, we begin to see how layering, materi­
al systems, and spatial organization can shape 
new types of learning environments.

The Centro Direzionale Subway Station in Naples, designed by Miralles 
Tagliabue EMBT and completed in 2025, is both a transport 
hub and an urban intervention. Covering about 10,000 (107,639 ft²)square 
meters, the station redefines the relationship between 
infrastructure and public space. Above ground, the project creates a new 
civic plaza with soft topographies, green areas, and ramps that 
ease the transition between different levels, turning what could have been a 
purely technical structure into a lively public realm. The roof 
is one of its most striking elements: a series of vaulted glulam timber arches 
that recall traditional railway stations while at the same time 
evoking natural, organic rhythms. Skylights are cut into these vaults, filter­
ing daylight down into the underground concourses. Below, 
the walls and ceilings are clad with metallic finishes in Pompeii-inspired 
tones, referencing Naples’ layered cultural history. The station’s 
structure combines lightweight wooden vaults with the stability of concrete 
and steel, a dialogue between old and new materials. 
Together, the roof, ground, and structure create a fluid, integrated environ­
ment where circulation, light, and atmosphere blend 
seamlessly, transforming a metro stop into a civic landmark that feels both 
contemporary and deeply rooted in place.

In this studio, we use the fundamentals of ar­
chitectural design through geometry, form, and 
spatial organization, focusing on how simple 
operations can generate complex spatial ideas. 
As students, we begin by working with basic 
objects, folding and translating them in 3D 
space to record their movement as trajectories. 
These trajectories are then developed into rib­
bon models, which help us visualize motion, 
structure, and interior space while introduc­
ing concepts like massing, ground, section, 
and datum. Through this process, we learn 
how 2D information can be transformed into 
three dimensional form and spatial systems. In 
the second half of the course, we apply these 
ideas to a real site by studying Los Angeles 
State Historic Park, using on-site observation 
and measurement to inform our design. Build­
ing on our earlier ribbon studies, we develop a 
proposal for a public pool and training facility, 
exploring how curved geometries can trans­
late into architectural form while responding 
to the site and program. The project ultimately 
brings together geometry, spatial thinking, and 
real-world context into a complete design pro­
cess.

This course introduces visual studies through 
hands-on material experimentation and fab­
rication, focusing on how texture, color, and 
material systems can shape design at differ­
ent scales. As students, we work in teams to 
test processes like papier-mâché and casting, 
learning how to mix paper pulp with glue to 
create a material that is strong enough to sup­
port weight. At the same time, we experiment 
with plaster and silicone molds to study sur­
face, texture, and detail through casting. These 
smaller tests lead into a larger final project, 
where we design and build a full-scale chair 
using cardboard as the main structure. We 
then apply layers of pre-mixed colored papi­
er-mâché to the surface, reinforcing the struc­
ture while also developing its visual quality. 
Through this process, the course emphasizes 
making, problem-solving, and the connection 
between material experimentation and final 
form.

gray foam and zip ties. These were meant to be fast and intuitive—the zip ties al­
lowed us to pull and tension the foam into organic shapes we couldn’t have planned

To find our final look, we experimented with colored papier-mâché made from wet 
tissue and glue. By layering the pulp, we created these vibrant, tactile swatches. 
Some are rough and fibrous, while others have a rippled, skin-like texture once dry. 
These tests became our primary reference for how color and material would behave 
on the full-scale model.

through the process of moving from generated 
images to spatial and visualized models. As stu­

The exterior of Villa Savoye is perfect white concrete, from a distance this build­
ing looks like a perfect white modern ship floating on green grass. Its façade looks 
seamless from the whole and exudes a feeling of near perfection, a feeling that gives a 
sense of timelessness and avant-garde purity that doesn’t feel like a building made in 
that era. The villa exemplifies Le Corbusier’s “Five Points of Architecture,” including 
pilotis, a flat roof terrace, ribbon windows, an open plan, and a free façade, making 
it a masterful display of modernist principles (Kroll). The entire villa is like a white 
box suspended from slender pillars. This bold design approach is more of an exquisite 
sculpture than a house.

will not feel the boundary between natural and man-
made objects. The building has an appearance that 
does not fit in with the surrounding landscape and 
environment, but its innovative large glass and lev­

the surroundings; it is more like levitating above the

the building warmth. The green grass seems to sepa­

dreamy living machine, indifferent and unassuming.
The structure of the Villa Savoye appears to be 
unaffected by gravity. The elevated structure of the 
ground floor gives the impression that the subject of 
the house is suspended in the air, a design that allows 
circulation between air and space. The columns are 
very thin and stand like delicate reeds, elegant and 
firm, allowing the plane of the house to be unrestrict­
ed by the ground, visually weakening the abruptness 
that the columns bring to the building itself. This sus­
pension of solidity gives the villa a weightless visual 
experience, as if it were not built but suspended.
In the interior of the villa, Corbusier still utilizes a 
minimalist style that matches the exterior. The spiral 
ramp, a key feature, guides visitors from the lower 
level to the open-air roof terrace. This design choice, 
in keeping with Corbusier’s concept of an “architec­
tural promenade,” slows the movement of inhabitants 
and offers a dynamic spatial experience (Kroll).

a means of connecting different planes of transporta­
tion, but also as a stylistic device, allowing the style 
of the entire house to form an integrated whole, a 
seamless unfolding of a picture, from the oppressive 
interior spaces to the freedom of the sky on the top 
floor.

Quote:
“ The Grande Arche is not merely a monument, but a portal through 
which we glimpse the continuum of time—a modern gateway to our col­
lective future.”
Keyword: Gateway
The word “gateway” perfectly describes the architecture of the Grande 
Arche and the nature it gives to La Défense in Paris. The Grande Arche is 
an interpretation of the classical arch and a modern reinterpretation of the 
Arc de Triomphe in the central area of Paris. The Grande Arche has been 
a striking landmark since its construction, and from the historic center of 
Paris towards the modern business district of La Défense it represents the 
entrance to the new city as a threshold. I chose “gateway” as a keyword 
because it represents the building as a gateway, which can function as a 
large entrance, but also connects the past and the future of the city in both 
directions, whether it be from the old city to the new city or in the oppo­

The Arch of Titus functioned as one of the earliest and clearest architec­

processions, but also visually connected key parts of the city through its 
axial alignment. The space surrounding the arch encouraged gathering, 
reinforcing its public and performative function. Its scale, central pas­
sage, and placement made it a hinge between everyday civic life and the 
sacred heart of Rome. The monument’s integration into the processional 
route gave it temporal and spatial meaning, it was a gate into a narrative 
space. There is a similarity in the exploration of the location of the Arc de

Located in the La Défense business district, with Paris’ most modern 
skyline, the Grande Arche forms the backbone of the area. Much like the 
Brandenburg Gate in downtown Berlin, it is not only a central point, but 
also a symbol of historical change and a spatial and physical connection 
point that organizes the surroundings by defining boundaries and open­
ing up views. In a deliberate alignment with the Arc de Triomphe and 
historic Paris, it translates visual and symbolic meanings from distance 
to time along a central axis; stretching through the centuries. The build­

squares and crossroads create intersections for the building; highlighting 
centrality. It does not compete with the environment, but reshapes it. As 
a gateway and a new landmark for the city, it not only divides space, but 
also connects history and architectural language in a contextualized way,

The gramde arche explores how buildings can serve more than just shel­
tering functions, they can shape how we experience culture, history, and 
social change. Louis Kahn’s essay Monumentality reflects this idea by 
defining monuments as structures that embody permanence and stir collec­
tive memory. For Kahn, a true monument must go beyond appearance—it 
should emotionally resonate and mark something larger than its practical 
use. In contrast, Adrian Forty’s Words and Buildings approaches the idea

of “threshold” as a spatial condition: a moment of transition, a link be­
tween inside and outside, or between civic and personal life. For Forty, 
thresholds are not just doors or passages—they’re zones of interaction 
that structure everyday experience. Though coming from different angles,

My case study, the Grande Arche in La Défense, Paris, captures this dual 
idea of monumentality and threshold. Positioned at the western end of

porous in experience. Like the Arch of Titus, it aligns with the city’s major 
axis and frames civic movement. Its form recalls the Arc de Triomphe, but 
instead of stone and sculpted relief, it uses steel and glass, presenting an 
abstract, modern reimagining of a national monument. The Grande Arche 
opens space rather than enclosing it—it invites circulation, both physically 
and symbolically. The visible steel framework and minimalist detailing 
contrast with the richly carved surfaces of structures like the Arch of Sep­
timius Severus, foregrounding a new kind of monument; one of openness 
and flow. Through these gestures, the Grande Arche doesn’t just echo 
history, it repositions it within a modern urban context.

What makes the Grande Arche powerful is how it expresses the threshold 
not only through form, but through its integration with the city. It chal­
lenges the traditional notion of monumentality. Instead of anchoring itself 
through heavy materiality or ornate symbolism, it uses flatness, transpar­
ency, and modern construction to suggest a future-facing vision. This calls 
into question Kahn’s idea of timeless permanence, proposing instead that 
monuments can be light, flexible, and inviting. At the same time, it ex­
pands on Forty’s idea of the threshold, turning it into a large-scale urban 
experience. It becomes more than an object—it becomes a connector of 
past and future, of tradition and innovation. In doing so, the Grande Arche 
reframes how we think of monuments today. It proves that monumentality 
and threshold are not only compatible; they can be combined into a sin­
gle architectural act that redefines how we encounter space, memory, and
